                                             Abstract
    This paper develops an architectural and training framework for causal-coding Transformer
neural networks based on a categorical mapping from linguistic universals to cognitive universals.
The guiding idea is that robust linguistic universals are weak but highly informative observable
traces of stable cognitive invariants. If one can learn an approximate cognition–frame–language
correspondence inside a Transformer, then linguistic universals can be used as training signals
for latent causal modules: they can help decide which heads and channels should be stable,
which dependencies should route through mediators, which context updates should commute,
which couplings are likely to be double-counting, and which parts of the model should be pruned
or protected.
    Formally, we model a causal-coding Transformer as an object of a quantale-enriched cate-
gory CTQ . Its modules are attention heads, value channels, feed-forward channels, adapters,
predictive-coding state nodes, or column-like blocks; its context-conditioned training steps are
endomorphisms Uc : Z → Z; and its causal gates are based on interventional influence. We then
introduce learned maps

                K
                b θ : CTQ → FrameQ ,      Ebθ : FrameQ → LangQ ,     b θ = Ebθ ◦ K
                                                                     Λ           bθ ,

where FrameQ is a category of semantic frames and LangQ is a category of TUG-style linguistic
cores. A linguistic universal is represented as a subobject U ,→ L in LangQ ; its cognitive
explanation is the pullback of U along Λ b θ , selected by a quantale-valued weakness criterion
that rewards fit and retention while penalizing cost, commutator defect, mediator leakage, and
double-counting.
   The main engineering thesis is this:

                       b θ = Ebθ ◦ K
                   Use Λ           b θ to regularize hidden Transformer updates
            so that causal modules, semantic frames, and linguistic externalizations
                           form approximately commuting diagrams.

Equivalently:

          Tune gates, pruning, routing, mediator heads, stability, and anti-forgetting
            using quantale weakness over fit, cost, commutators, mediator leakage,
                                        and double-counting.

The proposed losses include naturality losses, closure losses for hierarchy universals, mediator
residual losses for cross-module dependencies, commutator losses weighted by semantic-frame

      overlap, low-frustration externalization losses for word order, stability losses mapping high-
      stability universals into hard-kernel modules, and anti-double-counting losses over interventional
      footprints. The resulting framework turns category theory into a concrete recipe for causal-
      coding Transformer design.

1     Introduction
1.1    Why linguistic universals should help neural network design
A large Transformer trained only by next-token prediction is under strong pressure to discover many
useful linguistic regularities, but it is not explicitly told which internal structures should be reusable,
which should be protected, which should be mediated, and which should be pruned as redundant. A
causal-coding Transformer adds one important ingredient: it tries to identify which internal units,
heads, channels, or modules have genuine interventional influence, and it gates learning accordingly.
However, even causal coding needs guidance about the appropriate grain of modularity.
    Linguistic universals can provide exactly this kind of guidance. A robust linguistic universal is
not merely a surface regularity. In the perspective developed in the companion paper on linguistic
and cognitive universals, it is the observable shadow of a cognitive invariant transported through
semantic frames and linguistic externalization. That is, we have a correspondence of the schematic
form

    cognitive causal modules −→ semantic frames −→ linguistic forms and typological patterns.



                                                    3
If this correspondence can be learned or approximated inside a Transformer, then the linguistic
side gives a weak observable probe of the cognitive side. The model need not be forced to obey
a hand-coded grammar. Instead, linguistic universals become soft constraints on latent causal
organization.
    The central engineering idea of this paper is:

              Use linguistic and semantic-frame structure as weak observable probes
                  of the latent causal modularity we want a Transformer to learn.

This gives a principled way to structure and tune causal-coding Transformers.

1.2   From a philosophical correspondence to a neural objective
The earlier category-theoretic formulation of the linguistic–cognitive correspondence can be sum-
marized as

                    linguistic universals are direct images of cognitive invariants
                 along a weakest, quantale-enriched externalization correspondence,

and conversely
                       cognitive universals are weakest pullback explanations
                                   of stable linguistic subobjects.

For a neural network designer, this says the following.
    First, if the network contains a stable latent causal pattern, and if that pattern is relevant
to language, then some image of it should be visible through semantic-frame probes or linguistic
probes. For example, a stable person-role representation should help explain person hierarchy
regularities; a stable event-role representation should help explain argument-structure regularities;
a stable context router should help explain context-indexed local laws.
    Second, if a linguistic universal is robust across languages and contexts, then the weakest ade-
quate explanation of that universal should correspond to some latent causal structure. If the model
explains a robust hierarchy by scattering many redundant correlations across unrelated heads, that
explanation is not weak: it is expensive, brittle, and double-counted. A better model should place
the relevant structure in a stable module or small family of modules.
    Third, if a broad linguistic dependency crosses two modules, the model should not couple those
modules densely unless necessary. It should try to find a mediator. This is the neural version of
the linguistic theorem that nontrivial cross-module universals require mediation.

1.3   What this paper contributes
This paper turns those conceptual points into a concrete causal-coding Transformer design. The
main contributions are:

 1. A quantale-enriched category CTQ of causal-coding Transformers, whose objects contain mod-
    ular latent state, context update endomorphisms, do-influence gates, probes, and stability
    assignments.




                                                  4
 2. A learned cognition–frame–language map
                                       b θ = Ebθ ◦ K
                                       Λ           b θ : CTQ → LangQ ,

      where Kb θ reads semantic frames from Transformer modules and Ebθ predicts linguistic external-
      izations from frames.

 3. A set of training losses derived from categorical structure: naturality, pullback, closure, me-
    diator residual, commutator, double-counting, stability, low-frustration externalization, and
    context gluing.

 4. A theorem package showing, under explicit assumptions, that these losses push the model
    toward small context commutators, mediated cross-module dependencies, closed hierarchy rep-
    resentations, and lower double-counting.

 5. A practical training and evaluation protocol for testing whether category-guided causal cod-
    ing improves continual learning, multilingual transfer, pruning, head specialization, and inter-
    pretability.

1.4    The intended reader
The paper is written for two audiences. Readers interested in architecture can treat the category
theory as a precise design language for heads, gates, probes, routers, and regularizers. Readers
interested in mathematics can view the architecture as an approximate neural realization of a lax
monoidal, quantale-enriched correspondence between cognitive causal systems, semantic frames,
and TUG-style linguistic cores.
    Every formal definition is accompanied by a plain-language explanation. The goal is not to make
a Transformer into a symbolic grammar engine. The goal is to use the stable parts of linguistic
structure as weak constraints on the model’s latent causal organization.


2     Background
2.1    Causal coding in Transformers
A standard Transformer contains a rich supply of potential modules: attention heads, value chan-
nels, key-query subspaces, feed-forward neurons, low-rank adapters, MoE experts, residual stream
subspaces, and layer-wise prediction-error nodes in predictive-coding variants. Causal coding treats
these as candidate causal factors and asks which ones have interventional influence on predictions,
frames, or downstream internal states.

target r can be written schematically as

2.2   The Causal-Continual-Learning principle
Let Θ be parameter space and let each context c define a loss Lc and an update vector field

                                          Xc (θ) = −∇Lc (θ).

The commutator of two update fields is

                                    [Xc , Xd ] = DXd Xc − DXc Xd .

If [Xc , Xd ] is small, then training on c and then d is close to training on d and then c, and also
close to a joint update on the mixture. If all relevant commutators are small, sequential learning
has bounded path-dependence and bounded forgetting.
    The categorical version replaces vector fields by endomorphisms. A context update is a mor-
phism
                                              Uc : Z → Z
on a modular state object
                                          Z∼
                                           = Z1 ⊗ · · · ⊗ ZM .
If Uc acts nontrivially only on factors in Sc ⊆ {1, . . . , M }, and Ud acts only on disjoint factors Sd ,
then
                                          Uc ◦ Ud ∼
                                                  = Ud ◦ Uc .
The approximate version measures the failure of this equality by a metric or quantale-valued defect.

2.3   Linguistic universals as structural probes
The TUG account of linguistic universals distinguishes five robust structural types.

 1. Hierarchy universals behave like closure systems.

 2. Broad cross-module universals require mediators.

 3. Narrow word-order universals behave like low-frustration externalization systems.

 4. Universalhood is graded by stability rather than binary.

 5. Many genuine universals are context-indexed local laws.

   These are not merely linguistic facts. In the correspondence view, they are signatures of the
cognitive architecture that produces and understands language. A causal-coding Transformer can
therefore use them as weak labels for how its latent modules should be arranged.




                                                    6
2.4      Quantale weakness as a unifying tuning principle
A commutative quantale is a complete lattice (Q, ≤) with a monoidal product ⊗ distributing
over arbitrary joins. Quantales let us combine heterogeneous notions of cost, evidence, weakness,
confidence, and error.
   For a relation H ⊆ X × X interpreted as indistinction and a valuation µ : X → Q, define
                                             _
                                   W(H) =         µ(x) ⊗ µ(y).
                                                (x,y)∈H

Large W(H) means the hypothesis leaves many valued distinctions unmade. In ordinary Occam
terms, it commits to less. In neural-network terms, it avoids encoding unnecessary couplings.
   For Transformer tuning we use a product quantale such as
                                                         op       op     op
                    Qmtotal = Qmf it × Qmret × Qop
                                                mcomm × Qmleak × Qmdc × Qmcost ,

where fit and retention should be high, while commutator defect, mediator leakage, double-counting,
and cost should be low. The superscript op reverses the order on cost-like coordinates. A good
model is a maximal element of this product order, or an approximate Pareto point selected by a
scalarization or epsilon-constraint rule.

3.1      The three categories
We now define the categorical objects used by the architecture. The definitions are intentionally
flexible: different implementations may instantiate heads, channels, adapters, or columns as mod-
ules.

Definition 3.1 (The category of causal-coding Transformers). A causal-coding Transformer object
is a tuple
                      Th eta = (Z, {Zm }M

      or column;

    • each Uc : Z → Z is a context-conditioned update endomorphism;

    • Sc ⊆ {1, . . . , M } is the intended support of context c;

    • G = (gm,c ) is a causal-gate system derived from do-influence estimates;

    • Π is a family of probes and readouts;

    • ρm ∈ [0, 1] is a stability or rigidity assignment for module m;

    • µ is a quantale-valued valuation on modules, traces, or behaviors.

    Plainly: objects are not just trained networks, but trained networks equipped with a modular

Definition 3.2 (Semantic-frame category). An object of FrameQ is a structured semantic frame

                                 F = (R, A, E, C, ΓF , µF ),

    A semantic frame is a cognitive structure such as “agent acts on patient”, “speaker addresses

                                      L = (C` , Otyp , ⊥` , Γ` , µL ),

4.1   Hierarchy universals become closure losses and hard-kernel structure

4.2   Broad cross-module universals become mediator-head constraints
Suppose a proposed dependency links module family A and module family B. The TUG factoriza-
tion theorem says that if A and B are genuinely independent, no nontrivial cross-module universal
should hold without a mediator. The neural design rule is:

          Do not allow broad dependencies to be implemented as diffuse dense coupling.
                   Allocate explicit mediator heads or mediator channels instead.

   Let zA , zB , zM be latent states for the two endpoint modules and a mediator. The desired
condition is
                                           zA ⊥ zB | zM .
A direct information-theoretic penalty is

HSIC, CKA residuals, sparse regression residuals, or contrastive leakage predictors.
    The categorical version uses a residual. If A ⊗ M ≤ B is the desired implication in a quantale

                                            M ∗ = A ⇒Q B,

where ⇒Q is the right residual:

                               A⊗M ≤B            ⇐⇒      M ≤ A ⇒Q B.

In neural terms, the model should search for the smallest mediator capacity that explains the
dependency. Direct A ↔ B edges are pruned unless they produce evidence gain that cannot be
routed through M .

5     Architecture: the Category-Guided Causal-Coding Transformer
5.1   High-level architecture
The architecture can be described as follows:
                                        Input tokens
                                             ↓
                    Predictive-coding / causal-coding Transformer stack
                                             ↓
        LM head frame probes universal probes context router causal-gate estimator

Internally the Transformer stack is decomposed into modules:

                         Z∼
                          = Zmkernel ⊗ Zmmed ⊗ Zmext ⊗ Zmshell ⊗ Zmtask .

This decomposition is conceptual; implementation may realize it through heads, adapters, experts,
low-rank subspaces, or columnar blocks.

5.2   Module families
The following module families are useful.

Hard-kernel modules. These carry high-stability frame structures: person, number, basic argu-
   ment role, event role, quantification, core reference, and other closure-like invariants. They
   have low plasticity, strong retention regularization, and high pruning resistance.

Mediator modules. These implement bridges between otherwise factorized domains: syntax–
   morphology, semantics–syntax, discourse–reference, perception–language, action–language, and
   memory–language. They should be sparse, explicit, and monitored for leakage.

                                                   12
Externalization modules. These implement PF and linearization choices: word order, morphol-
   ogy packaging, positional conventions, local surface constraints, and low-frustration coherence.

Shell/adaptation modules. These carry medium- and low-stability patterns, including language-
    specific, domain-specific, speaker-specific, and task-specific residue.

Router modules. These select supports Sc for contexts, phrase types, discourse regimes, lan-
   guages, domains, or tasks.

  • dependency parses and morphological tags;

  • multilingual typological labels;

  • synthetic counterfactual interventions;

  • self-supervised consistency across paraphrase, translation, and context restriction.

8     Training procedure
8.1   Data sources
A practical system can use several kinds of signal.

 1. Ordinary language-modeling data for LLM .

 2. Multilingual corpora, translation pairs, and paraphrase pairs for observational equivalence and
    adjunction/cycle losses.

 3. Parsed corpora with dependency, role, morphology, or discourse labels for frame probes.

 4. Typological databases or hand-curated universals for closure, stability, and low-frustration
    losses.

 5. Synthetic counterfactuals and interventions for do-influence estimation.

 6. Continual-learning streams over language, domain, genre, task, or user context for commutator
    and retention evaluation.

8.2   A staged training recipe
One possible staged procedure is:

Stage 0: Pretrain. Train an ordinary Transformer or predictive-coding Transformer on language
    modeling.

Stage 1: Instrument. Choose a modular decomposition into heads, channels, adapters, experts,
    or columns. Add frame probes, universal probes, context routers, and causal-gate estimators.

Stage 2: Calibrate probes. Train K  b θ , Ebθ , and Λ
                                                    b θ using frame labels, parses, multilingual align-
    ments, paraphrases, and typological supervision.

Stage 3: Estimate do-footprints. Use interventions, patching, ablation, Jacobian-vector prod-
    ucts, or predictive-coding Hessian approximations to estimate Footm for modules.

Stage 4: Train category-guided causal gates. Update modules using task, frame, and lan-
    guage influence. Penalize double-counting and prune redundant paths.

Stage 5: Add commutator training. Train across context streams while penalizing frame-weighted
    commutator defects.

Stage 6: Consolidate. Move high-stability structures into hard-kernel or inner-shell modules.
    Move task-local residue to outer-shell adapters. Prune mediator leakage and redundant foot-
    prints.

9     Experimental program
9.1     A minimal experiment
A first experiment need not be huge. Train four models on a multilingual or multi-domain corpus:

 1. baseline Transformer;

 2. causal-coding Transformer;

 3. causal-coding Transformer with frame and universal probes;

 4. full category-guided causal-coding Transformer with naturality, closure, mediator, commutator,
    and double-counting losses.

     Use a small set of robust structures:
                       number hierarchy,     person hierarchy,    argument roles,
                          case/agreement,    clause type,   word-order relation.

9.2     Metrics
Evaluate:
    • language-modeling loss;

    • frame/probe accuracy;

    • closure violation rate;

    • forgetting under sequential adaptation;

    • hidden commutator defect;

    • linguistic/frame commutator defect;

    • do-footprint redundancy;

    • mediator leakage;

    • pruning efficiency;

    • transfer across languages and domains.

                                                   20
     The sharp prediction is not merely lower perplexity. It is:

               At comparable validation loss, the category-guided model should have
             lower commutator defect, lower double-counting, better pruning efficiency,
                               and lower continual-learning forgetting.

9.3    Intervention tests
The strongest evidence would come from interventions.

Closure intervention. Perturb a module representing a high hierarchy feature and test whether
    lower hierarchy features remain coherently represented.

Mediator intervention. Ablate the mediator and test whether the cross-module dependency
   collapses; ablate a direct endpoint coupling and test whether the mediator preserves behavior.

Commutator intervention. Train contexts in reversed order and test whether final performance
   changes less than in the baseline.

Double-counting intervention. Remove one of two heads with similar do-footprints and test
   whether loss changes little; remove a unique-footprint head and test whether loss changes
   more.

Stability intervention. Fine-tune on a domain shift and test whether high-stability modules drift
    less while low-stability modules adapt.


10      Implementation choices and variants
10.1     Which modules should be used?
The framework does not require one specific modularization. Reasonable module choices include:

  • attention heads;

  • value-channel groups;

  • FFN neuron groups;

  • low-rank adapters;

  • MoE experts;

  • residual-stream subspaces;

  • predictive-coding state nodes;

  • HBCML-style columns or microcolumns.

    A practical approach is to begin with coarse modules such as heads or adapters, then split
modules whose do-footprints show internal heterogeneity and merge modules whose do-footprints
are redundant.



                                                  21
10.2    How symbolic must the frame layer be?
The frame layer need not be fully symbolic. It may be:
  • discrete and symbolic, using explicit roles and feature predicates;
  • continuous, using learned frame embeddings;
  • hybrid, using symbolic roles with vector-valued fillers;
  • neural-symbolic, using a metagraph or typed graph as the interface.
   The categorical equations require only that frame transformations and linguistic transformations
be comparable through a loss. They do not require exact symbolic parsing at every step.

10.3    When typological labels are missing
If typological labels are unavailable, the system can still use proxy universals derived from data:
  • cross-lingual consistency constraints;
  • paraphrase equivalence classes;
  • dependency parse implications;
  • morphology co-occurrence hierarchies;
  • learned low-frustration graphs over externalization decisions;
  • context-local implication mining.
The framework is not limited to hand-coded universals. It can mine candidate universals and then
use weakness selection to decide which ones are worth turning into architectural constraints.

10.4    Quantale tuning in practice
In practice, the product-quantale objective may be implemented by a controller that adjusts λ-
weights. Let

11     Risks, limitations, and non-goals
11.1    Do not over-impose universals
The framework treats linguistic universals as weak constraints, not as hard-coded laws. Many
proposed universals are false, context-indexed, or statistical. The model should learn graded sta-
bility and context scope. Hard constraints should be reserved only for very high-confidence closure
structures.

                                                 22
11.2    Do not confuse probes with causes
A probe may detect a feature without the probed module causing that feature. This is why do-
footprints matter. A module should be considered a carrier of a universal only if it has interventional
influence on the relevant frame or linguistic readout.

11.3    Do not make the frame bottleneck too narrow
If K
   b θ is forced to capture all cognition in a small symbolic bottleneck, performance may suffer. The
frame layer should be an auxiliary organizing structure, not necessarily the only channel through
which language modeling proceeds.

11.4    The category theory is a design language, not magic
The categorical formulation clarifies what should commute, factor, pull back, or be mediated. It
does not by itself solve optimization. The empirical question is whether the corresponding losses
and architectures improve learning. The proposed experimental program is essential.


12     Conclusion
The linguistic–cognitive correspondence can be made operational for causal-coding Transformer
design. The core idea is that linguistic universals are weak observable traces of cognitive invariants.
If a Transformer learns an approximate map
                                            b θ = Ebθ ◦ K
                                            Λ           bθ

from hidden causal modules to semantic frames and linguistic externalizations, then linguistic
universals can be pulled back into hidden space as architectural and training constraints.
   The practical recipe is:

             Use Λ
                 b θ to regularize hidden Transformer updates so that causal modules,
                semantic frames, and linguistic externalizations form approximately
                                        commuting diagrams.

And:
            Tune gates, pruning, routing, mediator heads, stability, and anti-forgetting
              using quantale weakness over fit, cost, commutators, mediator leakage,
                                        and double-counting.
Hierarchy universals become closure losses and hard-kernel assignments. Broad cross-module uni-
versals become mediator-head constraints. Narrow word-order universals become low-frustration ex-
ternalization losses. Graded universalhood becomes module-stability scheduling. Context-indexed
laws become router-local constraints. Quantale weakness then chooses the weakest adequate expla-
nation: prefer causal mediators over redundant correlations, sparse support over diffuse entangle-
ment, and reusable kernels over brittle context-local residue.
    The resulting system is not a symbolic grammar bolted onto a Transformer. It is a causal-
coding Transformer whose latent modularity is shaped by the most stable observable traces of
human language. If the theory is right, such models should not merely be more interpretable. They
should forget less, prune better, transfer more cleanly across languages and domains, and discover
more reusable internal structure.
