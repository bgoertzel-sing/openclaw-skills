         From Singular Learning Theory to Predictive Accuracy
                 By Way of “Weakness as Evidence”
                                              Ben Goertzel

                                           December 9, 2025


                                                Abstract
          Singular Learning Theory (SLT) provides geometric invariants—most notably the local learn-
      ing coefficient (LLC), also called the real log canonical threshold (RLCT), denoted λ—that gov-
      ern the behavior of Bayesian evidence near singular minima. In a separate line of work, weakness
      theory establishes monotone relationships between a model’s “weakness” and its expected pre-
      dictive accuracy in withheld-link or withheld-truth evaluation protocols. This paper unifies
      these two frameworks. The key observation is that weakness, when defined as the posterior-
      predictive mass on a truth set, can be identified with a Bayesian evidence ratio. This identifi-
      cation allows SLT’s asymptotic machinery to propagate into statements about “soft accuracy”
      (posterior-predictive mass on truth), yielding characteristic O(λ/n) finite-sample corrections.
      We establish conditions under which these soft-accuracy results transfer to hard (0-1) accuracy,
      and argue that these conditions are typically satisfied in practical machine learning settings
      where models are designed to produce well-separated predictions. The framework provides a
      probabilistic, accuracy-facing interpretation of SLT quantities that complements standard log-
      loss formulations.


1     Introduction and Main Result
1.1   The problem: connecting geometry to accuracy
Singular Learning Theory (SLT) has emerged as a powerful framework for understanding Bayesian
learning in models whose parameter-to-prediction map has singularities—that is, models where
the Fisher information matrix degenerates at certain parameter values. Such singularities are
ubiquitous in modern machine learning: neural networks with redundant hidden units, mixture
models at boundaries of their parameter spaces, and many other commonly used architectures all
exhibit singular behavior.
    The central achievement of SLT is to characterize how these singularities affect learning. The
key quantity is the local learning coefficient λ (also known as the real log canonical threshold or
RLCT), a geometric invariant that measures the “effective dimension” of the model near a singular
point. SLT shows that λ controls the asymptotic behavior of the Bayesian evidence (marginal
likelihood) and, consequently, the expected generalization error—but traditionally, these results
are stated in terms of log loss (KL divergence).
    In many practical applications, however, the quantity of direct interest is not log loss but
rather an accuracy-like metric: link prediction accuracy in knowledge graphs, retrieval hit-rate
in recommendation systems, or the probability of correctly predicting withheld ground truths.
The question this paper addresses is: To what extent do SLT’s geometric invariants manifest in
accuracy-based evaluation metrics?


                                                     1
1.2   The challenge: from smooth to discrete
A fundamental tension underlies any attempt to connect SLT to accuracy. The quantities native
to SLT—free energy, KL divergence, log loss—are smooth and differentiable. Accuracy (0-1 loss),
by contrast, is a step function: it is discontinuous at decision boundaries. Improving the predicted
probability of the correct class from 0.6 to 0.9 dramatically improves log loss but leaves accuracy
unchanged (correct in both cases). Conversely, moving from 0.49 to 0.51 flips accuracy but barely
affects log loss.
    This discontinuity means that O(λ/n) scaling, which SLT establishes for smooth quantities,
cannot transfer directly to hard accuracy without additional structure. The relationship between
surrogate losses (like log loss) and 0-1 loss is mediated by inequalities (e.g., Pinsker’s inequality),
not equalities.
    Our approach addresses this challenge by working with an intermediate quantity: soft accuracy,
defined as the posterior-predictive mass on the truth set. This quantity is smooth (like log loss)
but accuracy-adjacent (it measures probability mass on correct answers). We show that SLT’s λ/n
scaling applies directly to soft accuracy. We then establish conditions—margin conditions—under
which soft accuracy controls hard accuracy, and argue that these conditions are generically satisfied
in practical settings.

1.3   The solution: weakness as a bridge
Our approach leverages weakness theory, which provides general results relating a quantity called
“weakness” to expected predictive performance. The central observation is that if we define weak-
ness as a posterior-predictive expectation—specifically, the posterior-predictive mass assigned to
a “truth set” H—then weakness becomes identifiable with a ratio of Bayesian evidence integrals.
Once this identification is made, SLT’s asymptotic expansions for evidence integrals yield corre-
sponding expansions for weakness.
   The logical chain proceeds as follows:
  1. Define a weakness quantity w(H) as the posterior-predictive mass on a truth set H (this is
     “soft accuracy”).

  2. In evaluation protocols where predictions are sampled from the model distribution, establish
     that expected accuracy equals soft accuracy.

  3. Identify weakness with a Bayesian evidence ratio via the posterior predictive distribution.

  4. Apply SLT evidence asymptotics to derive the scaling behavior of weakness.

  5. Conclude that soft accuracy corrections scale as λ/n (up to lower-order terms).

  6. Establish conditions under which soft accuracy controls hard accuracy, and argue these hold
     in practice.

1.5    Scope of the results
To be precise about what this framework establishes:
    Direct result: SLT’s λ controls the finite-sample behavior of posterior-predictive mass on
truth sets (soft accuracy). This provides an accuracy-adjacent interpretation of λ.
    Conditional extension: The transfer from soft accuracy to hard accuracy (0-1 loss) requires
margin conditions—that predictions be bounded away from decision thresholds. We argue in Sec-
tion 7 that these conditions are generically satisfied in well-designed machine learning systems.
    Scope limitation: In adversarial or pathological settings where predictions cluster near de-

cision boundaries, the soft-to-hard transfer may fail, and standard O(1/ n) rates would apply to
hard accuracy.


2     Weakness and Expected Predictive Performance
We begin by establishing the weakness-theoretic foundation. The goal of this section is to clarify
the relationship between weakness (soft accuracy) and expected accuracy under various evaluation
protocols.

2.1   The withheld-link evaluation paradigm
Consider the following common evaluation scenario. We have a set of objects U and a relation
H ⊆ U × U representing “true links” (or true positive pairs). Examples include:

   • In a knowledge graph, U is the set of entities and H is the set of true relations.

   • In a recommendation system, U is the set of users and items, and H encodes user-item
     preferences.

   • In a social network, U is the set of users and H encodes friendships or connections.

   A model is trained on a subset of the true links and evaluated on its ability to predict the
withheld links.

Theorem 2 establishes that in sampling-based evaluation protocols, expected hard accuracy exactly
equals soft accuracy. Many practical settings approximate this:

    • Retrieval systems that return items sampled according to predicted relevance scores.

    • Generative models evaluated on the quality of samples drawn from the model distribution.

    • Link prediction with probabilistic thresholding.

   For these settings, SLT’s control over soft accuracy translates directly to control over expected
hard accuracy.

3.3      The role of singularities
In regular statistical models—where the Fisher information matrix is positive definite everywhere—
the evidence has well-known Laplace approximation asymptotics with effective dimension d/2.
However, many models of practical interest are singular : the Fisher information degenerates at
certain parameter values, and the standard Laplace approximation breaks down.
    SLT addresses this by using tools from algebraic geometry to analyze the structure of the
singularities. The key insight is that even when the parameter-to-prediction map has complex
singular structure, the evidence integral still admits a controlled asymptotic expansion, but with a
modified effective dimension.

The central quantity in SLT is the local learning coefficient λ, also known as the real log canonical
threshold (RLCT).

7     Extension to Hard Accuracy
Theorem 1 applies to soft accuracy and to expected hard accuracy under sampling-based evaluation.
This section addresses hard accuracy under argmax (Top-k) evaluation.

7.1   The decision boundary problem
Under argmax evaluation, the model predicts the item(s) with highest µ(z). Accuracy is 1 if the
argmax lies in H, and 0 otherwise. This is a discontinuous function of the model parameters: small
changes in µ that shift the argmax across the boundary of H cause discontinuous jumps in accuracy.
    Soft accuracy wn (H) does not directly control whether the argmax lies in H. A model with
wn (H) = 0.9 might have its argmax outside H (if the remaining 0.1 mass is concentrated on a
single high-scoring false positive).

7.2   Margin conditions
The transfer from soft to hard accuracy requires a margin condition: the posterior predictive must
be bounded away from 0.5 (or more generally, from the decision threshold) on the truth set.

Definition 5 (Margin condition). A model satisfies a margin condition with parameter γ > 0 if,
for all z ∈ H,
                                                  1
                                    p(z | Dn ) ≥ + γ.
                                                  2
    Under such a condition, improvements in soft accuracy translate to improvements in hard
accuracy, and the O(λ/n) rate can transfer. This is analogous to the Tsybakov margin condition
in statistical learning theory, which enables fast O(1/n) rates for classification.

Proposition 1 (Soft-to-hard transfer under margin conditions). If the margin condition holds
uniformly over H with parameter γ > cλ/n for all sufficiently large n, then the O(λ/n) scaling of
soft accuracy implies O(λ/n) scaling of hard accuracy.

7.3   Why margin conditions typically hold in practice
While margin conditions are not guaranteed by SLT alone, there are several reasons to expect they
will be satisfied in practical machine learning settings.

1. Posterior concentration in well-specified models. In Bayesian inference, when the model
is well-specified (i.e., the true data-generating process lies within the model class), the posterior
concentrates around the true parameters as n → ∞. This concentration implies that posterior-
predictive probabilities for true items converge to their true probabilities, which are bounded away
from decision thresholds by definition. More precisely, if the true probability of item z ∈ H is
p∗ (z) > 1/2 + δ for some δ > 0, then posterior concentration ensures p(z | Dn ) > 1/2 + δ/2 for
sufficiently large n.

2. The structure of link prediction and retrieval tasks. In link prediction, the truth set
H consists of actual relationships (e.g., real friendships, verified facts, observed interactions), while
the complement consists of non-relationships. Models are specifically designed to exploit features
that distinguish true links from false ones—co-occurrence patterns, structural properties, semantic
similarity, etc. By construction, these features create a substantial gap between scores for true and


                                                   9
false items. A model that did not produce such separation would be useless for the task and would
not be deployed.
    Quantitatively, if a link prediction model achieves (say) 80% ranking accuracy, then for a typical
true link (u, v) ∈ H, the model scores (u, v) higher than 80% of false candidates. This ranking
separation implies a corresponding gap in probability space after normalization.

4. Hard cases are typically rare. Even when some items in H violate the margin condition,
these “hard cases” are typically a small fraction of the test set. Let Hhard ⊆ H be the set of items
with |p(z | Dn ) − 1/2| < γ, and let Heasy = H \ Hhard . If |Hhard |/|H| = o(1) as n → ∞, then the
overall accuracy is dominated by Heasy , on which the O(λ/n) scaling applies.
    Empirically, calibration studies of modern machine learning models consistently find that confi-
dent predictions (far from 0.5) substantially outnumber uncertain predictions (near 0.5) on typical
test sets. This is not coincidental: it reflects the fact that most test examples are “typical” instances
well-represented in training data, while only a minority are genuinely ambiguous.

5. The margin itself improves with n. As sample size increases, the posterior concentrates,
and margin parameters γ typically grow (or at least remain bounded away from zero). The condition
γ > cλ/n in Proposition 1 becomes easier to satisfy as n → ∞, since the right-hand side shrinks.
Thus, even if margins are small for finite n, they eventually dominate the λ/n correction term.
Remark 5 (When margins fail). Margin conditions can fail in adversarial settings: if an adversary
constructs test items specifically chosen to lie near decision boundaries, or if the truth set H is
defined to include only ambiguous cases. They can also fail in severely misspecified models where
the true data distribution places substantial mass near decision boundaries. These pathological
cases are important for worst-case analysis but are not representative of typical machine learning
deployments.

7.4   A refined statement
Combining Theorem 1 with the margin analysis yields:

Corollary 1 (Hard accuracy scaling under typical conditions). Under the hypotheses of Theorem 1,
if additionally:

  (i) The model is approximately well-specified, or

 (ii) The task structure ensures separation between true and false items, or

(iii) The fraction of test items near decision boundaries is o(1),

then expected hard accuracy under argmax evaluation admits O(λ/n) finite-sample corrections on
the “easy” subset of the test set, and overall accuracy corrections are O(λ/n) + o(1).

8    Discussion
                                                                                          √
1. The role of Bayesian averaging. The O(1/n) rate—faster than the typical O(1/ n) of
VC theory—arises from Bayesian averaging. The posterior predictive integrates over all parameter
values, smoothing the prediction landscape. This smoothing is what allows SLT’s analysis (which
targets smooth functionals) to apply. Empirical risk minimization with a fixed parameter estimate
                                                          √
would not enjoy the same smoothness, and standard O(1/ n) rates would apply.

2. Soft accuracy as the natural SLT target. Soft accuracy (posterior-predictive mass) is
the natural accuracy-like quantity for SLT analysis. It is smooth, it equals expected hard accuracy
under sampling-based evaluation, and it admits direct SLT asymptotics. Hard accuracy under
argmax evaluation is a derived quantity that requires additional (margin) conditions—but as argued
above, these conditions are typically satisfied.

3. Multiple strata and phase transitions. When multiple singular strata compete, SLT
predicts switching behavior driven by the tradeoff between the leading n` term and the subleading
λ log n term. In such cases, soft accuracy can exhibit stage-like transitions rather than smooth 1/n
decay. The O(λ/n) scaling applies within each regime but may not capture inter-regime transitions.

4. Connection to scaling laws. Modern deep learning research emphasizes scaling laws where
loss decreases as N −α with data or model size. The λ/n correction is consistent with this picture,
with the added nuance that λ is not constant—it depends on which singular stratum the model
occupies. A model finding a flatter minimum (lower λ) will have a smaller coefficient in front of
1/n, generalizing faster for the same data.

5. Weakness as an Occam’s Razor formalization. Identifying weakness with evidence ratios
connects to algorithmic information theory: the model seeks the lowest-complexity (λ) explanation
of the data. Lower λ implies both better compression (in the information-theoretic sense) and
higher soft accuracy on withheld truths.


9    Conclusion
By defining weakness as the posterior-predictive mass on a truth set H and identifying this with
a Bayesian evidence ratio, we have shown that SLT’s λ log n term propagates into soft accuracy,
yielding characteristic O(λ/n) finite-sample corrections. The framework establishes a clear chain
from SLT’s geometric invariants to accuracy-like quantities:

    • Direct: λ controls soft accuracy (posterior-predictive mass on truth).

    • Under sampling evaluation: Soft accuracy equals expected hard accuracy.

    • Under argmax evaluation: Soft accuracy controls hard accuracy when margin conditions
      hold—and we have argued these conditions are typically satisfied in practical settings where
      models are designed to produce well-separated predictions.

   This provides an accuracy-facing interpretation of SLT: the local learning coefficient λ governs
not only log-loss generalization but also, under typical conditions, the rate at which predictive
accuracy improves with sample size.
