Weakness as Local Evidence:
A Systems Theory for Degeneracy,
Refinement DAGs, and Continual Adaptation,
with Transformers as a Case Study
Ben Goertzel
December 6, 2025

Abstract
We propose a systems-level formalism for continual learning in nonstationary environments
that unifies three themes: (1) the local-learning-coefficient (LLC) view of degeneracy from singular
learning theory (SLT); (2) “weakness” as an Occam-like bias formalized in thermodynamic
quantales; and (3) causal-coding style modularization that makes continual learning robust by
enforcing approximate commutativity of context-conditioned updates.
The core technical insight is that it can be effective to define weakness to be the (local)
Bayesian evidence/volume object that SLT already analyzes. Under this definition, the LLC is
literally the asymptotic invariant of weakness in the large-sample (or low-temperature) limit. We
then show how tropicalization (log-sum-exp dominance) yields a min-plus semiring in which MAP
selection over a DAG of candidate factorizations becomes shortest-path dynamic programming.
This yields a clean mathematical model of “updating many approximate factorizations per
context”: quantale-message-passing over a refinement DAG.
We apply the framework to (i) stagewise degeneracy changes observed during standard
backprop Transformer training, interpreted as posterior switching between neighborhoods with
different LLCs, and to (ii) predictive/causal-coding Transformers, where causal gates and clarity
regularizers maintain a modular factorization that stabilizes continual learning under distribution
shift. Finally, we connect this to incremental compression: the refinement DAG can be viewed as
a DAG of compression programs, while causal-coding NNs implement those compressors. There
is also a connection to the TransWeave framework, which can be used to assist in adapting the
solutions to the dynamic-programming problem related to factorization as distributions shift.

Introduction

To approach AGI one must not be restricted to training models in one stationary regime. AGI
models or systems must continuously adapt across shifting domains, tasks, and user intents. A useful
theory should therefore describe not just a parametric model class, but a system: a coupling of (i) a
base learner, (ii) a structure controller that maintains a modular causal/factorized world-model,
and (iii) a meta-learner that keeps the whole mechanism stable under distribution shift.
The empirical clue: degeneracy changes. Recent work tracks a quantity called the "local
learning constant" (LLC) during Transformer training process and finds stage boundaries at critical
points of the LLC curve; these stages align with changes in internal structure and behavior. Their
SLT framing uses local free energy

The conceptual gap. LLC is usually computed as a geometric invariant of a singular statistical
model. Weakness, in contrast, is often introduced as an Occam-like preference for “indistinct”
hypotheses, sometimes formalized using quantales with soft inf-convolution. The gap in many
discussions is that weakness and LLC look related but live in different languages.
The main move. We define weakness to be exactly (local) evidence, i.e. the same integral
appearing in SLT. Then LLC is not approximated by weakness: it is the asymptotic log-volume
exponent of weakness. To study composition and control, we place this evidence object in a
thermodynamic quantale whose tropical (zero-temperature) limit becomes the min-plus semiring.
In that semiring, MAP over a refinement DAG of candidate factorizations is shortest-path dynamic
programming.
What this buys you.
• A single mathematical object (weakness as evidence) whose asymptotics yields LLC, and whose
algebra yields compositional control laws.

2.1

A Systems Architecture: Base Learner + Structure Controller
+ Meta-Controller
The three loops (and why they are distinct)

Informal picture.

We separate three time scales.

1. Inference loop (fast): infer latent activities given input and current parameters. In predictive
coding this is a relaxation dynamics that reduces free energy.
2. Learning loop (medium): update neural parameters (or program library) using local errors
and gates.
3. Structure/Meta loop (slow): update modular factorization, gating policies, clarity strengths,
and shift segmentation so as to keep the system in a regime where continual-learning guarantees
hold.
The thesis of this paper is that (3) can be formalized as inference and control on a refinement DAG,
and that LLC/weakness provides a quantitative signal for when refactorization is needed.

Formal system model

regimes). A nonstationary training process produces a time-indexed stream (ct , xt )t≥1 with ct ∈ C
and xt ∼ Pct .

Why multiple factorizations? A large model can represent many incompatible but locally-useful
ways of factorizing data. Different contexts may prefer different factorizations. Also, distribution

factorizations and update it online.
Definition 4 (System components). A system consists of:
• a base update rule Uc : Θ → Θ (neural or programmatic) for each context c;
• a structure controller that maintains a posterior (or score function) over f ∈ F;
• a meta-controller Φ that adjusts estimation/calibration/regularization/segmentation knobs so
as to keep the system stable under shift. (This is where TransWeave or other sophisticated AI
methodologies can help on the adaptive learning side.)

Singular Learning Theory: Local Evidence, Free Energy, and

3.1

Why local evidence (not global risk) is the right basic object

SLT explains generalization and learning dynamics in singular models by tracking how much
posterior mass sits near different neighborhoods of the parameter space. This is fundamentally a
volume computation, not a Hessian computation. Local evidence captures both loss (energy) and
degeneracy (entropy-like volume in parameter space). LLC appears as the exponent governing the
polynomial decay of evidence with sample size.

Watanabe’s local free energy expansion

For regular models, Laplace’s method yields Fn ≈ n`(ŵ) + d2 log n. For singular models, the “ d2 ”
becomes a rational number λ determined by the singular geometry. This λ is the LLC.

Weakness as Local Evidence in a Thermodynamic Quantale

4.1

Why quantales?

A system perspective needs a compositional algebra: how do modules compose, how do alternatives
compete, and how does “best explanation” emerge? Quantales provide exactly this: a lattice
of alternatives (joins) together with a notion of sequential composition (monoidal product) that
distributes over joins. Thermodynamic (soft-min) quantales model log-sum-exp dominance; their
tropical limit models hard-min composition.

Refinement DAGs: Many Approximate Factorizations and ContextDependent Selection

6.1

Why a refinement DAG captures “emergent causal/Bayesian structure”

A single static factorization is too rigid. A large learner can represent many decompositions;
contexts select among them. We represent the space of decompositions as a refinement DAG: edges
correspond to safer, incremental refactorizations (split a module; add a sparse interaction edge; etc.).
A prior over this DAG encourages simplicity (fewer edges, sparser cliques).

Why dynamic programming emerges

If every refinement step adds a local structural change (a “micro-compiler pass”) whose cost is
additive, then the total score of a factorization is a sum over edges along a path in the refinement
DAG. Minimizing a sum over paths is exactly a shortest-path problem. Over the thermodynamic
(soft) semiring it becomes log-sum-exp message passing; in the tropical limit it becomes min-plus
DP.

TransWeave and Bootstrapped Multi-Stratum Transfer for Causal
Factorization DAGs

8.1

Why TransWeave belongs in this story

TransWeave was originally developed as a general framework for transferring solutions between
structured dynamic-programming (DP) or Bellman problems. Each task is specified by a state space,
a Bellman operator, and a value function; transfer then amounts to learning an intertwining map
that approximately commutes with the Bellman operators of two tasks. The same mathematical
pattern appears naturally in our refinement-DAG picture:
• each distribution P or Q induces a DP problem over the refinement DAG
• the Bellman operator encodes the one-step refinement cost
• under distribution shift, we want to transfer structure from the “old” DP problem to the “new”
DP problem, using a map that approximately intertwines their Bellman operators.
This is exactly the niche TransWeave is designed to fill. In particular, the “Bootstrapped MultiStratum TransWeave” (BMS-TransWeave) algorithm provides a way to start from a coarse, lowquality mapping between the two DP problems and iteratively improve both the mapping and the
target solutions.
10

8.2

TransWeave in one paragraph (mathematical sketch)

At a high level, TransWeave treats each task T as a Bellman system (XT , BT ):
• XT is a space of value functions or policies (depending on the formulation);
• BT : XT → XT is a Bellman operator, e.g. (BT V )(s) = mina {cT (s, a) + γV (s0 )} or a min-plus
convolution on a graph.
A transfer map between two tasks T1 and T2 is an operator
T : XT1 → XT2
that approximately satisfies a Bellman–Darboux (BD) intertwining condition
T ◦ BT1 ≈ BT2 ◦ T .

(5)

Ideally, (5) ensures that if V1∗ is a (near-)fixed point of BT1 , then T V1∗ is a good approximate
fixed point of BT2 . TransWeave algorithms construct T by minimizing a BD loss derived from (5),
optionally combined with direct supervision on pairs of known solutions.

8.3

Refinement-DAG dynamic programming as a TransWeave task

In our refinement-DAG framework, each distribution over contexts induces a DP problem over the
DAG:
• nodes f ∈ F are candidate factorizations;
• directed edges e : f → f 0 are refinement moves (splitting modules, adding or removing sparse
interaction edges, changing gating structures);
• costs on edges encode the incremental change in the SLT-aligned score Sn (c, f ) = nL∗c,f +
λc,f log n + κ(f );
• the Bellman operator B is the min-plus DP update that propagates these costs forward along
the DAG .
For a given distribution P over contexts, let (F, BP ) be the corresponding DP problem, and VP (f )
its (approximate) value function over factorizations. For a shifted distribution Q, let (F, BQ ) and
VQ (f ) be defined analogously, with task-dependent costs reflecting L∗c,f and λc,f evaluated under Q
rather than P .
Under this identification, TransWeave’s BD map becomes a structure-transfer operator
T : XP → XQ
between value-function spaces over the same refinement DAG, satisfying
T ◦ BP ≈ BQ ◦ T .

8.4

Bootstrapped Multi-Stratum TransWeave for Causal Factorization DAGs
(BMSTW-CF)

Informal goal.

Under distribution shift from P to Q, we often have:

• high-quality refinement policies and value functions for (F, BP ),
• only low-quality or partially-trained policies/value functions for (F, BQ ).
The goal of BMSTW-CF is to iteratively improve both the transfer map T and the target-side value
function VQ by:
1. bootstrapping from low-stratum (weak) solutions on Q and better solutions on P ,
2. learning a BD map that fits these strata,
3. using this map to propose better initializations for Q and refine them with DP,
4. and repeating across strata until both T and VQ are of high quality.
Strata over the refinement DAG. We stratify factorization nodes f ∈ F by quality under the
new distribution Q. For instance, define a scalar score

and partition F into strata such as “barely acceptable”, “OK”, and “good” based on quantiles of

Initial approximate transfer map. Causal coding plus meta-control already provide a rough

• modules that retain high do-influence under Q are likely to correspond to modules that were
important under P ;
• mixed-Hessian/commutator surrogates identify interaction edges that survive vs those that
vanish under Q.
We use this information to define an initial coarse map
T0 : F → F,
for example by “copying” a factorization f from P to its closest structural analog under Q (same
module partitions, edges, and gates, where possible) and adjusting only obviously broken connections.

1. Low-stratum phase.
• Select a set of low-quality factorizations f ∈ F under Q and their parents/siblings.
• For each such f , use T0 to define a source-side partner fP that is good under P (value

8.5

Role of BMSTW-CF in handling distribution shifts

Upgrading a rough mapping to a high-quality one. Above we have argued that causal
coding plus meta-learning naturally produce a coarse, evolving picture of which factorizations in
the refinement DAG remain good under shift (small leakage, small commutators, small interaction
LLC corrections ∆λ). However, this picture is noisy and local. BMSTW-CF offers a principled way
to turn that coarse picture into a high-quality mapping and refined target-side policies:
• treat the DP problems induced by P and Q as Bellman systems (F, BP ) and (F, BQ );
• treat the causal-coding-induced correspondence as an initialization T0 between their valuefunction spaces;
• use BMS-TransWeave to learn an intertwiner T satisfying T ◦BP ≈ BQ ◦T while bootstrapping
better value functions on Q;
• thereby systematically upgrade the rough initial mapping into a better one that respects the
DP structure and the refined SLT/weakness scores.
Architectural placement. From the systems viewpoint we’ve outlined, BMSTW-CF lives in
the slow structure/metacontrol loop:
• the fast neural loop (in JAX or similar) performs predictive/causal coding inside whichever
factorization node is currently active;
• the medium loop updates parameters and local gates based on context-specific errors;
• the slow loop, implemented in a symbolic graph engine (e.g. MeTTa/MORK), maintains the
refinement DAG, runs DP over it, and uses BMSTW-CF to transfer and upgrade structures
as the distribution drifts.
This allows the system to reconcile the causal-coding local view (supports, leakage, commutators)
with the SLT/global view (LLC, weakness) in a computationally tractable way, across multiple
distributions and over time.

Causal Coding as Maintaining a Refinement DAG in a ContinualLearning Regime

9.1

Why causal coding belongs in the structure controller

Causal coding (CC) introduces two families of mechanisms: (i) causal gates that suppress updates
on edges with low do-influence, and (ii) clarity/pruning terms that remove redundant paths. From a
systems viewpoint, these are not just local heuristics; they are the operations that realize refinement
steps in the refinement DAG, and keep the model near a factorization state where context-conditioned
updates commute (or almost commute).

9.2

Abstract commutator control (CCL viewpoint)

Reminder of the commutator goal. The continual-learning theorems in causal-coding work
can be read as: If updates are local to supports (small leakage) and cross-support interactions are
sparse/small, then sequential training approximates joint training and catastrophic forgetting is
bounded.
Definition 26 (Approximate locality and commutator defects (schematic)). Fix a metric/enrichment
dΘ on endomorphisms of Θ. For each context c, let Uc : Θ → Θ be the realized update and Vc
an ideal support-local update. Define locality error εc := dΘ (Uc , Vc ). For a pair (c, c0 ), define
commutator defect ∆c,c0 := dΘ (Vc ◦ Vc0 , Vc0 ◦ Vc ).
Why this matters for the refinement DAG. Factorization states f determine support sets
and routing paths. Good factorizations make most pairs (c, c0 ) have small overlap and thus small
commutators. Distribution shifts threaten this by changing which modules matter, or by corrupting
influence estimation.
9.2.1

modules, and if the remaining cross-context interactions are sparse, then continual learning is
stable and path dependence is controlled by a sparse confusion graph. In practice, the difficult

under distribution shift: influence estimates drift, gates flip, leakage cascades across depth, and

with an explicit objective that punishes the very quantities that break the theorems: locality error
and commutator/confusion defects. This is exactly the move advocated in the distribution-shift
addendum, which proposes learning calibration/thresholds/clarity/segmentation policies so as to

Computational Scaling and an Implementation Sketch (Neural
+ Symbolic Kernel)

12.1

How big are refinement DAGs likely to be?

The space of all partitions/graphs over K modules is enormous (super-exponential). A workable
system must represent only a sparse, structured subset: hierarchical refinements, sparse interaction
edges, and per-context gating patterns. In that regime, the refinement DAG can be polynomial in
K.
A simple scaling model. Let K be the number of physical modules we expose (heads, channel
groups, etc.). Assume: (i) each module participates in at most d interaction edges in the sparse
interaction graph; (ii) refinements are hierarchical splits with branching factor at most b; (iii) we
keep at most M candidate factorizations per “region” of context space. Then nodes scale like
|F| ≈ O(M K) to O(M K log K) in typical hierarchical schemes, and edges |E| ≈ O(|F|b).
Toy to frontier-scale orders of magnitude. For toy Transformers, K can be ∼ 102 ; keeping
M ∼ 102 candidates gives |F| ∼ 104 . For medium systems, K ∼ 103 and M ∼ 103 yields |F| ∼ 106 .
For frontier-scale systems, K depends on granularity (heads vs head-groups vs block-level); coarser
granularity keeps K in the low thousands, making |F| in the 106 -107 range plausible.

12.2

Why split implementation (e.g. JAX for NN, MeTTa/MORK for DAG
control)?

The refinement DAG controller is symbolic/graphical: dynamic programming, min-plus message
passing, discrete segmentation, and constraint propagation. This is naturally expressed in a
reduction-kernel / logic substrate. The base learner is differentiable and batched: natural for
JAX/accelerators. Hence a split architecture:
• JAX: PC inference, gated weight updates, influence estimation (or its surrogates), LLC
estimators.
• MeTTa/MORK: maintain refinement DAG, run tropical/thermodynamic DP, manage priors
and segmentation policies, store and update the sparse confusion graph.
